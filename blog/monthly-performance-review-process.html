A monthly review that takes 20 minutes

Annual reviews fail because they ask managers to remember a year. Monthly reviews only ask them to remember a month.

Published 10 August 2026 · Updated 10 August 2026 · Merik team · 5 min read

A monthly performance review is a twenty-minute conversation about the last four weeks, grounded in what was actually recorded rather than what anyone remembers. It replaces the annual review's central problem — reconstructing twelve months from the most recent six weeks — with twelve small, accurate records. By the time an annual summary or increment decision is needed, it writes itself from notes that already exist.

Key takeaways
  • Annual reviews suffer from recency bias: managers recall the last six weeks and call it a year.
  • Four questions, twenty minutes, once a month. Anything longer will be skipped by March.
  • Ground it in logged work — what was delivered, what was blocked — not in impressions.
  • Write three or four lines per review. Twelve of those make the annual review a compilation, not an excavation.
  • Separate the performance conversation from the pay conversation, or only one of them gets heard.

Why annual reviews go wrong

Three well-documented failure modes, all structural:

  • Recency bias. Asked in March about the year, a manager retrieves February and January. Ten months of solid work compress into "generally fine".
  • Fabricated precision. Rating someone 3.4 out of 5 on "collaboration" implies a measurement nobody made. The number carries authority the evidence does not support.
  • Too much at stake in one meeting. When the review determines the raise, the promotion and the year's narrative simultaneously, both people manage the meeting rather than have the conversation.

Monthly reviews dissolve all three, mostly by lowering the stakes of any single conversation.

The four-question format

Twenty minutes, once a month:

  1. What did you deliver this month? Open with the record — the task log — not with memory. Five minutes.
  2. What got blocked or slower than expected, and why? This surfaces process problems, and the honest answer is often "I was waiting on someone", which is not a performance issue at all. Five minutes.
  3. What are the two or three things for next month? Specific enough to check in four weeks. Five minutes.
  4. What do you need from me? Ask it, then stop talking. Five minutes.

Question four is the one managers skip and employees value most. Silence after asking it is uncomfortable and productive — the useful answer usually arrives after the pause.

Grounding it in what actually happened

The difference between a useful review and a pleasant chat is whether it opens with evidence. If your team keeps a daily task log, you already have the month in front of you: what was worked on, for which client and project, how long it took, and what was blocked.

What that changes:

  • No reconstruction. Neither person has to remember week two.
  • Quiet contributors become visible. Steady delivery that never generated a crisis is exactly what memory-based review misses.
  • Blockers are already recorded. The pattern is visible rather than anecdotal.
  • Disagreements become checkable. "It felt like that took ages" versus what the log says is a productive conversation.
Reviews should start with the record and then discuss it — not start with an impression and then look for evidence supporting it.

Write three lines, not three pages

After each review, record: what was delivered, what needs to change, and what was agreed for next month. Three or four lines. Two minutes.

The payoff is compounding. Twelve monthly notes make the annual summary a compilation exercise rather than an act of archaeology, they give the increment cycle a documented basis instead of an impression, and if a performance issue ever needs formal handling, the contemporaneous record already exists. Long-form review documents, by contrast, get written once, in December, and are useful to nobody.

Keep pay out of the monthly conversation

If every monthly review might affect pay, employees will manage the conversation defensively and you will stop hearing anything real. Keep the monthly review about work, and hold compensation to its own cycle with its own meeting.

This does not mean the two are unconnected — twelve months of monthly notes should be the primary input to the increment decision. It means the employee knows which conversation they are in, which is what makes candour possible in the monthly one.

Where AI can help — and where it must not

Drafting is a reasonable use: given a month of logged tasks, a model can produce a first summary of what someone worked on. That saves a manager ten minutes of assembly.

Two conditions make this acceptable. First, it is a draft that a human edits — nobody should receive an assessment their manager did not write and does not stand behind. Second, it is grounded in real logged work, not generating plausible-sounding performance language from nothing.

What AI must not do is decide. Ratings, increments, promotion and exit are judgements a person is accountable for. The full boundary is drawn here.

Frequently asked questions

How often should performance reviews happen?

Monthly, in a short twenty-minute conversation, works far better for small teams than a single annual review. It removes recency bias, keeps feedback close to the work it refers to, and turns the annual summary into a compilation of notes that already exist rather than a reconstruction of the year.

What questions should a monthly performance review cover?

Four: what did you deliver this month, what got blocked or took longer than expected and why, what are the two or three priorities for next month, and what do you need from me. Five minutes each. The last question is the one managers most often skip and employees most often value.

How do you avoid recency bias in performance reviews?

Base the review on a record rather than on memory. If the team logs daily work with client, project, time and blockers, the month is in front of both people and neither has to recall week two. Reviewing monthly rather than annually also shortens the window that memory has to cover.

Should performance reviews and salary discussions be separate?

Yes. If every monthly conversation might affect pay, employees manage it defensively and honest discussion stops. Keep monthly reviews about the work and hold compensation to its own cycle — while still using the accumulated monthly notes as the primary input to increment decisions.

What should be written down after a performance review?

Three or four lines: what was delivered, what needs to change, and what was agreed for next month. That is enough to make the annual summary a compilation, to give the increment cycle a documented basis, and to have a contemporaneous record if an issue ever needs formal handling.

Can AI write performance reviews?

AI can draft a summary from a month of logged work, saving a manager assembly time, provided a human edits and stands behind it. It should not produce assessments from nothing, and it should never make the decision — ratings, increments, promotions and exits are judgements a person must be accountable for.
