Payslip format: what every payslip must show

A payslip that can't be checked by the person receiving it isn't a payslip — it's an announcement.

Published 31 July 2026 · Updated 10 August 2026 · Merik team · 6 min read

A payslip must show enough for an employee to verify their own pay without asking anyone. That means three blocks: identification (who, which period, which days were payable), earnings (each component and the gross), and deductions (each one itemised, leading to net pay). If any figure on the slip cannot be reconstructed from the other figures on the slip, the format is incomplete — and every gap becomes an HR query.

Key takeaways
  • Three blocks: identification, earnings, deductions — ending in a net pay figure the employee can arrive at themselves.
  • Always show payable days and days actually paid. Without them, no salary variation can be explained.
  • Itemise every deduction. A single "deductions" number is the fastest route to a disputed payslip.
  • Show year-to-date figures where you can — they save enormous time at tax-declaration season.
  • Issue the slip in the same run that calculates the pay, so the slip and the payment can never disagree.

The three blocks of a payslip

Every good payslip, regardless of country or industry, has the same skeleton:

  1. Identification — who is being paid, by whom, for which period, and on what attendance basis.
  2. Earnings — every component that adds up to gross pay.
  3. Deductions — every amount subtracted, leading to net pay.

The test of a format is arithmetic closure: gross minus total deductions equals net, and each of gross and total deductions equals the sum of its own listed lines. If an employee has to take any number on faith, that number will generate a question.

Block 1 — Identification and the attendance basis

Include:

  • Company name and address, and the employee's name, employee code, designation and department.
  • Pay period (month and year) and the pay date.
  • Payable days in the period — the working days after holidays and weekly offs.
  • Days paid — present, paid leave and approved WFH, plus any half-day fractions.
  • Loss-of-pay days, stated separately.
  • Bank account (masked) and payment mode, plus statutory identifiers such as PF/UAN or ESI numbers where they apply.

The attendance lines are the ones small businesses most often omit, and they are the most valuable. When someone's salary is lower than last month, the answer is almost always in these three numbers. Without them printed, every variation becomes a conversation. How LOP days translate into an amount is worked through here.

Block 2 — Earnings, component by component

Typical components, each on its own line:

  • Basic salary — the anchor for most statutory calculations.
  • House rent allowance (HRA).
  • Conveyance / transport allowance.
  • Special or other allowance — usually the balancing figure.
  • Variable pay, incentives or commission for the period.
  • Overtime, with the hours shown, not just the amount.
  • Arrears, clearly labelled with the period they relate to.
  • Reimbursements — keep these visually separate, since they are not salary and are usually not taxable in the same way.

Two rules save the most trouble. First, label arrears with their period — "Arrears (Apr–May)" prevents an employee thinking their base salary changed. Second, show overtime hours, so the amount is checkable. How these components relate to the CTC number on an offer letter is explained here.

Block 3 — Deductions, itemised

Common deduction lines:

  • Provident fund (employee contribution).
  • ESI (employee contribution), where applicable.
  • Professional tax, where the state levies it.
  • TDS / income tax deducted for the month.
  • Loan or advance recovery, ideally with the outstanding balance shown.
  • Loss of pay, if you treat it as a deduction rather than reducing earnings directly — pick one approach and stay consistent.

Never combine these into one "deductions" figure. An itemised block answers questions before they are asked; a single number guarantees they will be. Where the employer also contributes — PF and ESI typically — showing the employer share separately (clearly marked as not deducted from the employee) helps employees understand their total cost to company. What applies to your business depends on headcount and state — checklist here.

Year-to-date figures and why they earn their space

A YTD column beside the monthly figures — gross paid, tax deducted, PF contributed so far this financial year — costs you nothing to print and saves hours in January and February, when every employee is trying to reconcile their tax declaration. It also lets someone spot an error in month seven instead of at year-end, when correcting it is far more painful.

Format, delivery and retention

Practical points that matter more than they sound:

  • One payslip per employee per period, generated by the same run that calculated the pay. If the slip is produced separately from the calculation, the two will eventually disagree.
  • Delivered to the employee directly — emailed, or downloadable from their own login. Payslips forwarded through managers are a privacy problem.
  • Retrievable later. Employees need old payslips for loans, visas and rentals, often years afterwards. If the only copy is an email attachment, you will be regenerating them by hand.
  • Immutable once issued. A correction should produce a revised slip that says it is revised, not a quiet replacement of the original.

On statutory obligations: several Indian states, under their Shops and Establishments rules, require wage slips to be issued and wage registers to be maintained in prescribed forms and retained for a set period. Requirements vary by state and by the applicable act, so confirm what applies to you and take local professional advice — this is general guidance, not legal advice.

Frequently asked questions

What must a payslip include?

A payslip should include the company and employee identification, the pay period and pay date, the attendance basis (payable days, days paid and loss-of-pay days), every earnings component adding up to gross pay, every deduction itemised, and the resulting net pay. Year-to-date figures and statutory identifiers such as PF or ESI numbers should be shown where they apply.

What is the difference between gross salary and net salary on a payslip?

Gross salary is the total of all earnings components — basic, allowances, overtime, incentives and arrears — before anything is subtracted. Net salary, or take-home pay, is what remains after deductions such as provident fund, professional tax, income tax and any loan recovery. The payslip should let an employee add up the earnings lines to reach gross, subtract the deduction lines, and arrive at net.

Why should a payslip show payable days?

Because it is the only way an employee can explain a change in their salary. If pay is lower than last month, the reason is almost always fewer payable days in the month, loss-of-pay days, or a half-day conversion. Printing payable days, days paid and LOP days answers the question before it becomes an HR query.

Should deductions be itemised on a payslip?

Yes. A single combined deductions figure is the most common cause of disputed payslips, because the employee has no way to check it. List provident fund, ESI, professional tax, income tax and any loan or advance recovery separately, and show the outstanding balance on recoveries where you can.

Is it mandatory to give employees a payslip in India?

In practice yes for most employers. Wage slip issuance and wage register maintenance are required under state Shops and Establishments rules and other applicable labour legislation, with prescribed forms and retention periods that vary by state and by which act applies to your business. Confirm the specific requirement for each state you operate in and take local professional advice.

How long should payslips be kept?

Long enough to meet the record-retention period in the labour legislation applicable to you, and practically, long enough to serve employees who need old payslips for loans, visas or rentals years later. Keeping them retrievable from the employee's own login avoids regenerating them by hand each time someone asks.
