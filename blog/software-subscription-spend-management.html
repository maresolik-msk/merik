Finding the software seats nobody uses

Nobody decides to waste money on software. It accumulates one forgotten seat at a time, charged monthly, forever.

Published 9 August 2026 · Updated 10 August 2026 · Merik team · 5 min read

SaaS waste in a small business is almost never one bad decision — it is dozens of small ones nobody revisits. A seat kept after someone left. A trial that converted. A tool two people championed and nobody uses. Because each charge is small and monthly, none of them trigger a review. The fix is a register of every subscription with seats tied to named employees, plus a fifteen-minute monthly routine.

Key takeaways
  • Seats must be tied to named employees, not counted in aggregate — "10 seats" tells you nothing about who left.
  • Offboarding is the biggest leak. The account is disabled; the paid seat quietly continues.
  • Track renewal dates and notice periods. Auto-renewal with a 30-day notice window is where the money actually goes.
  • Convert everything to a monthly cost per tool so annual and monthly plans can be compared honestly.
  • One fifteen-minute monthly review typically removes 20–30% of spend in the first pass.

How the waste accumulates

Five mechanisms, all mundane:

  • Departed employees' seats. IT disables the login; nobody reduces the seat count. You pay for that person indefinitely.
  • Trials that converted. Someone trialled a tool on a company card, it auto-converted, and the charge is small enough to never be questioned.
  • Duplicate capability. Three overlapping tools because three teams each chose their own. Each is individually justified.
  • Tier drift. You upgraded for one feature during one project, and stayed on the higher tier for two years afterwards.
  • Auto-renewals with notice periods. An annual plan renews because the cancellation window closed 40 days before a date nobody had recorded.

Each is small. Twenty of them, at a typical small business, is a meaningful fraction of an employee's salary.

Building the subscription register

Per tool, track:

  • Tool name and what it's for — one line. This is what exposes duplicates.
  • Owner — a named person accountable for the tool, not a department.
  • Plan and tier.
  • Billing model — per seat, flat, or usage-based.
  • Seats purchased and seats assigned, with assignment to named employees.
  • Cost and currency, plus the billing cycle.
  • Normalised monthly cost — annual ÷ 12, so everything is comparable.
  • Renewal date and notice period.
  • Payment method — this is how you find the subscriptions nobody told you about.

Populate it from your card and bank statements for the last twelve months, not from memory. The statement is the only complete list; every business doing this for the first time finds at least one tool nobody remembered.

Tie seats to people, not to a count

"Design tool: 10 seats" is not manageable information. "Design tool: 10 seats — Anita, Ravi, Priya, Meera, and 6 unassigned" is immediately actionable, and it makes offboarding mechanical.

Once seats have names attached:

  • Departures produce a specific list of seats to reclaim, per tool.
  • The gap between purchased and assigned becomes visible without an audit.
  • New requests can be filled from unassigned seats before buying more.
  • Renewal conversations start from the number of seats you actually use.

Fix the offboarding leak first

This is the highest-return single change. Add software seats to your offboarding checklist alongside asset return and access revocation:

  1. List every tool the departing employee holds a seat on.
  2. Disable the account and reduce the seat count or reassign the seat — these are two different actions and only the first usually happens.
  3. Transfer ownership of anything they owned inside the tool: files, dashboards, automations, admin rights.
  4. Record the date, so a later invoice can be checked against it.

Step 3 is the one that causes pain months later, when a critical automation stops working because it ran under a deactivated account. The wider exit sequence is here, and hardware return belongs in the same checklist.

The fifteen-minute monthly routine

  1. Scan the statement for charges not in the register. Add or cancel each one.
  2. Check seats assigned vs purchased for the top five tools by cost. Reduce where the gap is persistent.
  3. Review renewals in the next 60 days, so notice windows are not missed.
  4. Ask one question about the most expensive tool — is the tier still right, and would anyone notice if it were downgraded?

Fifteen minutes a month. The first pass typically removes 20–30% of spend at a small business that has never done it; after that it is maintenance, and it stays small precisely because it happens.

Negotiating at renewal

Renewals are where the register pays off, because you arrive with facts:

  • Know your real usage. Asking for a reduction from 20 seats to 13 is a normal conversation when 13 is what is assigned.
  • Diarise the notice period, not the renewal date. A 30-day notice window on an annual plan means the decision date is a month earlier than the calendar suggests.
  • Ask about annual pricing for tools you are certain about — usually 10–20% cheaper — and stay monthly for anything you might drop.
  • Consolidate. If two tools overlap, pick one at renewal. This is the only moment where switching cost is naturally lowest.

Frequently asked questions

How do you track software subscription spend in a small business?

Build a register listing every tool with its owner, plan, billing model, seats purchased and assigned, cost normalised to a monthly figure, renewal date, notice period and payment method. Populate it from twelve months of card and bank statements rather than from memory, because statements are the only complete list.

Why do companies pay for unused software seats?

Mostly because departing employees' accounts are disabled without the seat count being reduced — two separate actions, of which only the first usually happens. Trials that auto-converted, tiers upgraded for a single project, and duplicate tools chosen by different teams account for most of the rest.

How much can a small business save on SaaS?

A business that has never systematically reviewed its subscriptions typically finds 20 to 30% of spend is removable in the first pass — unassigned seats, forgotten tools and unnecessary tiers. After that it becomes maintenance, and the figure stays small precisely because the review happens regularly.

What should be on a software offboarding checklist?

List every tool the employee holds a seat on, disable the account and separately reduce or reassign the seat, transfer ownership of anything they owned inside the tool such as files, dashboards, automations and admin rights, and record the date so a later invoice can be checked against it.

When should renewals be reviewed?

Diarise the notice deadline rather than the renewal date. An annual plan with a 30-day cancellation window has a real decision date a month before the calendar renewal, and missing it commits you to another full year. Reviewing renewals falling in the next 60 days each month catches these reliably.

Is annual or monthly SaaS billing better?

Annual billing is usually 10 to 20% cheaper and is worth taking for tools you are confident about keeping. Stay on monthly billing for anything you might drop, anything recently adopted, or anything whose usage you have not yet measured — the discount is not worth being locked into a tool you stop using in month three.
